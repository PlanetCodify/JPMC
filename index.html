Context

International Consumer Bank is scaling a branchless, digital-only retail model across markets — UK live since 2021, Germany publicly targeted for 2026. Every new market ships more gen-AI-adjacent features, right as a real regulatory gap opened underneath all of them.

On April 17, 2026, the Federal Reserve issued SR 26-2, superseding SR 11-7, and explicitly excluded generative and agentic AI from formal model-risk scope for banks over $30B in assets — JPMC clears that threshold many times over. Separately, a live MRGR job posting scoped to ICB lists only traditional quantitative-model skills: financial math, probability theory, numerical methods. No mention of gen-AI, LLMs, or agentic systems anywhere in it.

Read together, those two facts say the same thing from opposite directions: the expertise to govern this exists. The entry point doesn't.

Problem

The obvious fix — stand up a new governance function for gen-AI — is also the wrong one. MRGR already has the regulatory relationships, the examiner trust, and the review discipline this needs. Building a parallel function next to it doesn't close the gap, it duplicates the exact thing that's already there and adds a second, less-trusted process for the bank to maintain.

The harder problem sits one layer down. Research on comparable internal-platform populations shows 64% of engineers bypass a platform that's slower than just building the workaround themselves. A governance layer that's technically correct but slow to integrate against doesn't get used — it gets routed around, and the SR 26-2 gap stays exactly as open as it was before anyone built anything.

Approach

The first version of this platform proposed a full customer data, communications, and CMS build, with AI governance bundled in as one feature inside it. A structured review across engineering, design, executive, legal, research, and skeptic perspectives flagged that as backwards: it bet the largest investment on the least-validated assumption in the whole plan — whether a governance function even wants standardized tiering routed into it.

The rebuild inverts that. Ship the tiering layer alone, first. Validate it against a real, named function — MRGR — before any bigger platform investment follows. If MRGR doesn't want the routing, the platform's job shrinks to a much smaller, cheaper build. If it does, everything else has a foundation actually worth building on.

Outcome

Still concept-stage — nothing here has shipped to a real organization. Modelled against the PRD's own targets: a market engineer gets a working classification response in under a business day, MRGR accepts and reviews the majority of what's routed to it, and the platform can't pass its own bar by being fast but going unused — a 90-day sustained-use check is a required gate before any further investment, not an afterthought metric.

How the system actually behaves

The mechanics behind the headline claim — what the classifier is required to do, how the architecture is structured, what a reviewer actually sees, and where a seven-perspective review found it wrong.

Behavior Specification

Task — classify a gen-AI or agentic feature request into one of three risk tiers and return the required review path, routing Tier 3 into MRGR's real queue rather than a parallel process this platform invents.

Inputs
Feature description, data sources touched, customer-facing surface, whether it influences a credit or account decision
Constraints
Never auto-approves anything touching a credit or account decision. Self-routing logic for credit-adjacent features requires a fair-lending (ECOA/Reg B) review before this ships — the classifier doesn't get to decide its own jurisdiction over that category.
Disallowed
Silent approval of anything the classifier is uncertain about — ambiguous input defaults to Tier 2, never Tier 1. No fail-safe if the classifier itself is unavailable also defaults to Tier 2, logged, never a silent failure.
Latency budget
P50 under 2 seconds, P95 under 10 seconds — synchronous enough that an engineer never files a ticket and waits

System Architecture

Everything inside the boundary is what gets built. MRGR's own review process sits outside it on purpose — this platform calls into it, it doesn't redesign it.

Where It Fell Short — Not Just What Passed

A seven-perspective structured review (engineering, design, executive, legal, research, skeptic, customer voice) ran against the first full draft. It found real problems, not just polish notes:

The primary metric was gameable.Time-to-first-integration alone rewards a fast handshake, not sustained use — a team could hit the target with one throwaway test call and never touch the platform again. Fixed by adding 90-day sustained use as a co-primary metric, specifically so speed can't stand in for adoption.
The integrator and reviewer experiences were assumed, never designed.The whole adoption thesis rested on an integration path that was never actually specified — no docs, no sandbox, no error handling. Same gap on the reviewer side: nothing described what an MRGR reviewer would actually see. The prototype's MRGR Queue tab exists specifically because this was flagged as missing, not nice-to-have.
A real liability gap in the classifier's self-routing logic.The system decides its own review tier for features that could touch fair lending law (ECOA/Reg B) — and nothing confirmed the existing model-risk process Tier 3 depends on actually covers gen-AI outputs. Added as an explicit Phase 1 blocker, not a footnote.
Failure Analysis — The Turn

What failed — the original plan bundled AI governance as one feature inside a full customer data, communications, and CMS platform build.

Why — that bet the largest, most expensive part of the investment on the single least-validated assumption in the whole thesis: whether a governance function even wants standardized tiering routed into it. If that assumption was wrong, the bigger build would have been wasted underneath it.

What changed — a direct question ("how much of this already exists at JPMC?") surfaced a real finding: a Model Risk Governance & Review function already exists, scoped to ICB, for traditional models. The platform's entire positioning inverted around that — from "build new governance" to "build the door into governance that already exists." Phase 1 now ships the tiering layer alone, validated against MRGR directly, before any bigger platform investment follows it.

AI Ethics & Risk

The governance model exists because customers are already weighing this trade-off, whether the bank instruments for it or not. Published research: 64% of consumers want personalization, but only 39% think it's worth the privacy cost. 52% worry AI could mistakenly freeze their account. 62% say they'd lose confidence in their bank after a data breach.

Default-deny, not default-allow.Ambiguous classification and classifier downtime both default to Tier 2, never silently to Tier 1 — the system is required to escalate its own uncertainty, not hide it.
No self-governance on credit decisions.The classifier can never auto-approve a feature touching a credit or account decision, under any circumstance. That category always reaches a human, by construction, not by policy that could be quietly relaxed.
Fair-lending review is a gate, not a formality.Before the classifier's self-routing logic ships, an ECOA/Reg B review is required — a system that decides its own jurisdiction over credit-adjacent features is exactly the kind of thing an examiner would probe first.
